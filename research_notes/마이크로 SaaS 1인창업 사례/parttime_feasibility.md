# Part-Time SaaS/Software Founders While Holding a Demanding Primary Commitment (2023–2026)

## What documented cases exist of founders building software part-time (under ~10–15 hrs/week) while working full-time or in an intensive study program?

### Takeaway
There is a real but thin body of documented cases — mostly self-reported on Indie Hackers, Hacker News threads, and founder blogs/newsletters — of people building SaaS or small software products on 5–15 hours/week while employed full-time. Direct, verifiable cases of people doing this during an intensive clinical/medical program (med school, residency, dental school) are essentially absent from public write-ups; the closest material is physician/entrepreneurship advice columns describing a *recommended* pace rather than a documented case with revenue numbers.

### Cited Findings
- A founder built "Gaggle Mail" starting in 2015, reportedly working on it during his commute for 1.5–4 hours/week, eventually growing it to close to $30k MRR (timeline to that level not specified in the source snippet) — [Indie Hackers roundup via search](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/passive-income-saas-on-the-side-4ae31fb641)
- Jim Harrison grew a productized design service to $9k MRR in under 1.5 years while working a full-time job — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/services/growing-a-productized-service-to-9k-mrr-in-1-5-years-while-working-a-full-time-job-nRO5mMvO3vf0ChlkEuZH) (note: this is a productized *service*, not pure software/SaaS, and the underlying post could not be directly fetched — full text unverified, relying on search-result summary)
- An indie hacker employed at PayPal spent 2+ hours per night building side projects over roughly 1 year and 10 months across 8+ attempts before reaching about $800 MRR — [First Money Substack profile of Nikita Jerschow via search](https://firstmoney.substack.com/p/nikitajerschow) (direct fetch was blocked by network egress restrictions; content is from search-engine summary only, so treat as lower-confidence/unverified against primary text)
- One indie-hacker schedule pattern cited repeatedly in community advice: roughly 2 hours every weekday morning plus a few hours on Saturday, totaling ~10–14 hours/week — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-to-juggle-a-full-time-job-with-a-side-project-dccffa2bbd)
- Community guidance explicitly states a side project requiring 40 hrs/week "is essentially another job," and recommends choosing something that can move meaningfully on 5–10 hrs/week, at least at the start — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-to-juggle-a-full-time-job-with-a-side-project-dccffa2bbd)
- No verifiable, named case was found of someone building and monetizing a software product specifically during med school, dental school, or residency clinical rotations. Physician-focused advice sites discuss the *general practice* of combining clinical training with startup building, but without a specific person + product + revenue figures case study — [KevinMD (snippet only, direct fetch blocked)](https://kevinmd.com/2025/08/how-to-balance-clinical-duties-with-building-a-startup.html); [ResidencyAdvisor (snippet only, direct fetch blocked)](https://residencyadvisor.com/resources/medical-startups/no-you-dont-need-to-quit-medicine-to-build-a-real-startup)
- Recommended "sustainable" pace for a physician founder in Year 1: maintain 0.8–1.0 FTE clinical workload, do startup work nights/weekends plus one dedicated "build" block per week, with Year 1 goals being to validate the problem, build a basic prototype, and get 5–10 real users (not revenue) — [ResidencyAdvisor](https://residencyadvisor.com/resources/medical-startups/no-you-dont-need-to-quit-medicine-to-build-a-real-startup)
- That same source names physician-founders (e.g., in informatics and digital dermatology) who built companies without quitting medicine on day one, but gives no hours/week or time-to-revenue figures for them — [ResidencyAdvisor](https://residencyadvisor.com/resources/medical-startups/no-you-dont-need-to-quit-medicine-to-build-a-real-startup)

### Inferences
- The "case study" genre for this specific intersection (intensive clinical training + solo SaaS building) is largely unwritten/undocumented publicly — it's plausible people do this, but they are not writing it up with numbers the way indie-hacker-job people do. This is itself informative: it suggests either (a) it's rare enough not to produce a visible success story, or (b) people in these programs who do build something don't have bandwidth/incentive to also publicize it with metrics.
- Full-time-job-plus-side-project cases (non-medical) are much better documented, suggesting the reference case (dental student) would be looking at an even less time-rich, less flexible-schedule version of an already-hard pattern — full-time jobs have fixed hours and evenings/weekends off, while clinical rotations can have unpredictable hours, overnight call, and mandatory attendance that a 9-to-5 job doesn't.

### Gaps
- Could not find a single verifiable, named case of a med/dental/law student or resident building a SaaS product to meaningful revenue during their program, with corroborating evidence (dashboard screenshots, public revenue numbers). This is a real gap in available public information, not just a search failure — multiple targeted queries (Reddit, IndieHackers, general web) turned up only general advice/philosophy pieces, not case studies with numbers.
- Direct primary-source text for IndieHackers.com, KevinMD, ResidencyAdvisor, Hacker News, and Substack was blocked by the research environment's network egress proxy; all findings from these domains rely on search-engine-generated summaries/snippets rather than the original article text, which lowers confidence and means quotes/details could not be directly verified.

## How long did it realistically take part-time founders to reach first revenue, and to reach a meaningful amount (e.g., $500–2000/month)?

### Takeaway
Among part-time/side-project founders broadly (not specifically clinical-program students), typical timelines cluster around several months to first revenue and roughly 9–18 months to reach $1,000–3,000 MRR when working in the 5–15 hrs/week range — but the data is self-reported, survivorship-biased (people who failed or made $0 rarely write it up), and ranges are wide.

### Cited Findings
- General indie-hacker benchmark: most reach their first $500 MRR within 3–6 months of launching; the full path from idea to $3,000 MRR typically takes 9–18 months for a solo founder working ~10 hours/week — [search-aggregated Indie Hackers data](https://www.indiehackers.com/)
- Many indie hackers treat $3,000 MRR as the threshold where they increase weekly hours from 10 to 20, or begin planning a transition off full-time employment — [search-aggregated Indie Hackers data](https://www.indiehackers.com/)
- Benchmark distribution for time-to-$1,000 MRR across (mostly full-time) indie hackers: ~5 months at the top-outlier/25th percentile pace, ~8 months at the 50th percentile, ~12 months at the 75th percentile; one source states "on average it takes 9 months" and another "the average time to first $1K MRR is 7.3 months" (these are inconsistent with each other, suggesting different underlying samples/methodologies) — [search results, multiple aggregator blogs, sourcing unclear/unverified primary data](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/it-takes-5-months-to-reach-1k-in-mrr-491742f806)
- Individual rapid-outlier cases exist (e.g., "$1,000 MRR in 72 hours," "$1K MRR in 24 hours on launch day") but these are generally driven by pre-existing audience/distribution, not representative of a from-scratch part-time builder — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/1-000-mrr-in-72-hours-i-stopped-building-products-and-started-building-distribution-4fd06cf554)
- One case reached $1,000 MRR in 94 days — [Baremetrics blog](https://baremetrics.com/blog/friendly-growth-study)
- One case went from $0 to $1K MRR in 8 months as a solo dev — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/from-0-to-1k-mrr-in-8-months-bootstrapping-habit-pixel-as-a-solo-dev-684b6c056d)
- In a case combining part-time and near-full-time effort, a founder's co-founder did 1–2 days of client work and 4–5 days on the product (often including weekends) to reach $1K MRR — i.e., even this "fast" case involved far more than 10–15 hrs/week — [search-aggregated, source article unclear](https://www.indiehackers.com/)
- Jim Harrison's productized service reached $9k MRR in <1.5 years while holding a full-time job, but this is a service business (his own labor delivering the service) rather than a self-serve SaaS product, which changes the time-investment profile (ongoing service delivery hours vs. product-building hours) — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/services/growing-a-productized-service-to-9k-mrr-in-1-5-years-while-working-a-full-time-job-nRO5mMvO3vf0ChlkEuZH)
- One blog aggregator (unverified as primary data, appears to be an SEO content site rather than a survey) states solo founders "typically" work 10–30 hrs/week in months 1–6 and 20–40 hrs/week in months 6–18 of a project — [buildmvpfast.com via search snippet, low confidence source](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/saas-only-way-out-indie-hacker-dream-desperation-2026)

### Inferences
- Across sources, a rough consensus emerges: first (non-trivial) revenue often comes within a few months of launch, but climbing to a "meaningful" $500–2,000+/month typically takes somewhere between 8 months and 1.5–2 years for people working part-time, and faster paths usually involve either an existing audience/distribution channel, a service-labor component, or effort well above 10–15 hrs/week.
- Given the wide variance and self-selection in what gets published (people who hit $0 don't write case studies), the "typical" time-to-meaningful-revenue for someone strictly capped at under 10 hrs/week, with no prior audience, is probably at the longer end of these ranges (12+ months) or may never arrive at all — but no source directly quantifies a "success rate" or base rate of failure to caveat the survivorship bias.

### Gaps
- No source provides a denominator (how many part-time founders who *start* a project reach any given revenue milestone vs. abandon it) — all figures found are success-story numerators. This is an important caveat for feasibility assessment but not resolved by available search results.
- No source specifically times a clinical-training-intensity case (where "hours available" itself is in the 5–10/week range with clinical rotations, rather than gradually increasable as one's income need to grow).

## Did any of these founders have their free time shrink mid-project (e.g., promotion, new semester), and how did that affect the project — stall, quit, or push through?

### Takeaway
Community discussion confirms this is a common and recognized failure mode — momentum loss following life/job changes (promotions, new jobs, new babies, moving) is widely discussed on Indie Hackers as a cause of stalled or abandoned projects — but concrete, named before/after case studies with specific numbers on how a shrinking-time event affected a specific product's trajectory were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Indie Hackers community members widely report that it's "difficult to build momentum when releases happen infrequently," citing patterns like shipping "once every 2 months" while working part-time, and that hustle-driven initial momentum can "crash" at some point — [Indie Hackers, synthesized from search results](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/whos-hustling-who-s-building-calmly-indie-hackers-weigh-in-dc728300b3)
- Community discussion names concrete life-change triggers for momentum loss: starting a new job, quitting a job, and (implicitly) reduced context-switching capacity; founders who moved to working on projects full-time reported faster progress and feedback loops, which they said helped sustain motivation compared to the part-time phase — [Indie Hackers, synthesized from search results](https://www.indiehackers.com/)
- One counter-pattern: a case exists of someone continuing to build ("a $12k/mo AI chatbot generator") while traveling with a new baby — i.e., not all major life-capacity changes caused a stall, though this is a single anecdote without hours/week detail — [Indie Hackers stories database, via search](https://www.indiehackers.com/stories)
- One indie hacker explicitly renegotiated with their employer to cut from 40 to 32.5 hours/week specifically to protect time for their side project, i.e., proactively defending against time shrinkage rather than reacting to it after the fact — [Indie Hackers, via search summary](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-to-manage-side-project-with-day-job-3tOPXnSFTrZPWcaMHLP0)
- A separate (and opposite) failure pattern reported: some side-project attempts failed not from time shrinkage per se but because founders "weren't enjoying their full-time job and their personal life was negatively affected," suggesting time-scarcity and burnout interact rather than being independent variables — [Indie Hackers, via search summary](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-to-manage-side-project-with-day-job-3tOPXnSFTrZPWcaMHLP0)

### Inferences
- The available evidence supports "stalling" (reduced release cadence, loss of momentum) as the more commonly reported outcome of shrinking free time, rather than a clean "quit" or "push through" — projects seem to enter a slow-drip maintenance mode more often than being explicitly killed or heroically accelerated.
- No evidence was found either confirming or denying that an increase in commitment intensity (e.g., dental clinical rotations) reliably kills projects outright; the community discourse treats this as a real risk to plan around (e.g., proactively negotiating hours, time-blocking) rather than a foregone conclusion.

### Gaps
- No documented before/after case study was found showing a specific product's growth curve/MRR trajectory bending at the exact point a founder's available time dropped (e.g., a chart showing MRR flatlining after a promotion or new semester). This is the single biggest gap relative to the key question as posed, despite multiple targeted search attempts (search terms included combinations of "promotion," "new job," "new semester," "stalled," "quit," "momentum").
- No case was found of a student in an intensive academic program (as opposed to a full-time job) hitting a mid-program time crunch (e.g., new semester, boards exam, start of clinical rotations) with a documented project outcome.

## Is there general advice/data in the indie-hacker community about what's realistic for "a few free months now, busier later" founders specifically?

### Takeaway
There is no advice found that is framed exactly around the "currently free, will get busier" scenario, but closely analogous guidance exists in two forms: (1) general indie-hacker advice to launch/validate fast (within weeks, not months) before committing years of part-time effort, and (2) physician-entrepreneurship advice that explicitly sequences ambition to available capacity (validate now, build to real traction only when capacity allows), which maps reasonably well onto a "front-load validation while time exists, then sustain at low intensity" strategy.

### Cited Findings
- Pieter Levels' widely cited advice is against spending years or many months on a prototype — he recommends a maximum of about one month for a prototype, with most of his own products shipped and validated within 24–72 hours, on the logic that time should be front-loaded into validation via shipping, not extended building — [levels.io, via search summary](https://levels.io/startups)
- Levels also advises building solo (not with co-founders) and doing the basics (code, design) yourself, on the grounds that coordination and delegation cost more time than they save for a very time-constrained builder — [levels.io, via search summary](https://levels.io/startups)
- Physician-entrepreneurship advice recommends *not* pursuing medicine and entrepreneurship sequentially but concomitantly (in parallel, at low intensity) starting early, rather than waiting for "free time" that may never reliably arrive — the explicit Year-1 target is validating the problem, building a basic prototype, and getting 5–10 real users — not revenue — while clinical FTE stays at 0.8–1.0 — [ResidencyAdvisor, via search summary](https://residencyadvisor.com/resources/medical-startups/no-you-dont-need-to-quit-medicine-to-build-a-real-startup)
- General community guidance explicitly frames the choice as picking a side-project scope that "can move meaningfully on 5–10 hours a week, at least at the start," implying that if hours will later shrink further, the initial project scope should be calibrated to the future (lower) hours budget, not the current (higher) one — [Indie Hackers, via search summary](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-to-juggle-a-full-time-job-with-a-side-project-dccffa2bbd)

### Inferences
- Synthesizing the closest available analogues (Levels' "validate fast, don't over-invest months into pre-revenue building" philosophy, and physician-advice's "start now at sustainable low intensity rather than waiting," plus the community norm of scoping for 5–10 hrs/week as a floor) suggests the most evidence-aligned strategy for a "free now, busier later" founder is: (a) use the current free months to rapidly validate/ship something small rather than build a large product, since a few free months is closer to Levels' "weeks, not months" prototyping window than to the 9–18-month timelines needed to reach meaningful MRR; (b) pick a product/scope sized to be maintainable on the post-rotation-intensification hours budget (closer to 5 hrs/week or less), since scoping to current free time and then having to shrink later is the failure pattern the community explicitly warns about; (c) treat "meaningful income" ($500–2,000/month) as a 1–2 year-plus horizon even under favorable part-time conditions, and longer/uncertain under a clinical-rotation-constrained schedule, based on the indie-hacker benchmark ranges above.
- No source directly validates or refutes this synthesized strategy for the specific dental-student case — it is an inference from adjacent guidance, not a cited direct recommendation.

### Gaps
- No indie-hacker community post or survey was found that specifically addresses the "currently have free months, will have much less time later" planning problem as its own named topic (as opposed to general "how to balance a side project with a full-time job" advice, which assumes roughly constant time availability).
- No quantified data exists on success/revenue rates for founders who explicitly planned around a known future time cliff (e.g., a known return-to-school date or rotation start date) versus those who didn't.
