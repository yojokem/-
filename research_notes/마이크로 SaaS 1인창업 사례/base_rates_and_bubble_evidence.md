# Base-Rate Statistics and "Bubble vs Real" Evidence for Micro-SaaS / Indie Software (2024–2026)

Note on source quality throughout this file: almost none of the numbers below come from peer-reviewed academic research or a single authoritative census. The micro-SaaS/indie-hacker space is measured mostly by (a) self-reported community surveys (Indie Hackers), (b) blog-level aggregations of scraped Stripe/public data by third-party bloggers (not academically audited), and (c) general startup-failure statistics (CB Insights, Failory) that are about VC-style startups broadly, not specifically solo/micro-SaaS makers. Every such distinction is flagged inline.

## What percentage of indie SaaS/micro-SaaS products ever reach meaningful revenue ($1K/$10K MRR)?

### Takeaway
Across several independent (but methodologically weak/non-academic) aggregations, a majority of listed indie products make $0, and only a small single-digit percentage ever cross $10K MRR — the shape is consistent across sources even though none of them is a rigorous study.

### Cited Findings
- A ScrapingFish-style analysis of Stripe-verified Indie Hackers products found that **54% make exactly $0 in revenue**, and this 54% zero-revenue rate was reportedly unchanged from 2023 to 2024 — [Indie Hacker MRR distribution analysis, as summarized in search results](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/saas-only-way-out-indie-hacker-dream-desperation-2026) (self-reported/aggregator claim; underlying methodology not independently verified in this research pass — flagged as single-source estimate).
- Of the 46% of Indie Hackers products that do make money, the same analysis breaks it down as: **~25% sub-$1K MRR** (hobby tier), **~15% $1K–$10K MRR**, **~5% $10K–$100K MRR**, and **~1% $100K+ MRR** — [same source](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/saas-only-way-out-indie-hacker-dream-desperation-2026) (single-source, not independently corroborated).
- The same analysis claims **the top 5% of indie founders capture 70%+ of total revenue**, with the next 40% splitting the remainder and the bottom 54% capturing nothing — a power-law distribution, not a normal one — [source](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/saas-only-way-out-indie-hacker-dream-desperation-2026) (single-source estimate).
- A separate community estimate on active Indie Hackers: **50% of active indie hackers make under $1K/month; 20% make $1K–$10K; 10% make $10K–$100K; under 5% make $100K+** — [stealwhatworks.com, via search summary](https://stealwhatworks.com/blogs/news/indie-hackers-over-10k-per-month) (single-source blog estimate; direct fetch of this page was blocked by network egress restrictions during this research, so only the search-engine summary could be verified).
- Median monthly revenue across all listed products on Indie Hackers was reported as around **$30** — [sideprojects.dev, via search summary](https://sideprojects.dev/blog/indie-hacker-analysis) (single-source estimate; likely reflects the heavy concentration of $0 and near-$0 listings).
- A broader subscription-app benchmark (not indie-hacker-specific): among **more than 115,000 subscription apps**, only **4.6% of new apps reach $10K in monthly revenue within two years** — cited in aggregate search summary but the primary publisher (likely RevenueCat or a similar subscription analytics firm) was not directly reachable in this pass; treat as an industry-analytics estimate rather than an academic study. [Source as surfaced in search](https://www.jenariusganlary.com/blog/how-much-indie-hackers-actually-make).
- One individual indie hacker's published four-year ledger: **26 projects launched over 4 years, generating $115K total revenue combined; 18 of the 26 projects made exactly $0** — [buildmvpfast.com summary of the ledger](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/indie-hacking-real-numbers-4-years-26-projects-115k-2026) (this is a single individual's self-reported anecdote/case study, not a market-wide statistic, but it is a concrete, named, quantified data point consistent with the aggregate "most products make $0" pattern).
- General micro-SaaS survey claim: **70% of micro-SaaS businesses earn under $1K MRR**, and only **18% reach the $1K–$5K range** — [search-engine summary citing rockingweb.com.au / related micro-SaaS analyses](https://www.rockingweb.com.au/18-month-rule-micro-saas-startup-failure-analysis/) (blog-level aggregation, not an academic survey; direct fetch was blocked).
- The community platform **State of Indie Hackers 2025** exists as a "community-driven survey & report capturing tools, revenue, and stories," but its specific numeric findings could not be extracted in this pass (the site was returned as a search result but not independently fetched/verified) — [stateofindiehackers.com](https://stateofindiehackers.com/) — flagged as a **gap**, see below.

### Inferences
- Despite differing methodologies, three independent aggregations (ScrapingFish/buildmvpfast, stealwhatworks, sideprojects.dev) converge on the same rough shape: **roughly half of listed indie products make $0**, a large second tier makes under $1K MRR, and only a small single-digit percentage (~5%) reach $10K+ MRR. This convergence across separately-run, non-academic analyses is suggestive but should not be treated as statistically validated, since all ultimately draw on overlapping self-listed/Stripe-connected Indie Hackers data rather than a random or representative sample of "everyone who tried."
- Because listings on Indie Hackers are self-selected (people who choose to publicly list a product and often connect Stripe), the true population of "everyone who ever attempted a micro-SaaS" is almost certainly worse than these numbers suggest — those who never got far enough to list a product, or who quietly abandoned before connecting Stripe, are likely undercounted.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or government/academic dataset was found that specifically measures micro-SaaS/indie software revenue outcomes; everything is community self-report or third-party blog aggregation of Indie Hackers/Stripe data.
- The exact methodology behind the widely-repeated "54% make $0" and "4.6% of subscription apps reach $10K MRR in two years" figures could not be verified at the primary source in this research pass (several candidate domains — solooperatorstack.com, stealwhatworks.com, techstartups.com, thesaascfo.com, appfigures.com, thenextweb.com, stackoverflow.blog, indiehackers.com, buildmvpfast.com — were blocked by the network's egress proxy during fetch attempts, so only search-engine-generated summaries of these pages could be used, not the full original text). This is a meaningful limitation: these should be treated as secondary paraphrases, not confirmed primary-source quotes.
- State of Indie Hackers 2025's actual published statistics were not retrievable in this pass.
- No data found specifically isolating "micro-SaaS" (small recurring-revenue software products) from the broader "indie hacker" category (which includes content sites, newsletters, physical products, etc.), so some cited percentages may include non-SaaS side projects.

## What does the revenue distribution look like — is it heavily skewed?

### Takeaway
Every source found describes the distribution as a steep power law (not a bell curve): a large mass at or near $0, a long thin tail of moderate earners, and a tiny number of outsized winners who capture most of the aggregate revenue.

### Cited Findings
- "The distribution of outcomes is a power law, not a normal curve. The winners win big, and the 54% who make zero are invisible." — [buildmvpfast.com / solooperatorstack.com summary](https://solooperatorstack.com/blog/indie-hacker-revenue-distribution-tam-clarity/) (paraphrased from search summary; direct fetch blocked).
- Top 5% of indie founders reportedly capture 70%+ of total measured revenue, consistent with power-law concentration — [same source as above].
- Starter Story (a platform aggregating founder case studies, acquired by HubSpot in Feb 2026) advertises **$10,842,423,564+ in total revenue across its 4,483+ case studies** and **"$4B+/mo in combined revenue"** across the platform — [starterstory.com, via search summary](https://www.starterstory.com/) — this is explicitly a survivorship-biased, curated database of *successful* founders (the platform's entire premise is showcasing businesses that reached meaningful revenue), so these huge aggregate numbers should be read as evidence of the winner's-tail size, not as representative of typical outcomes.
- Failory and related startup-failure aggregations note that even among funded/VC-style startups, lack of product-market fit (cited in ~42% of failures in one aggregation) and cash-flow problems (~29%) dominate failure causes — [search summary citing rockingweb.com.au / Failory-adjacent sources](https://www.rockingweb.com.au/18-month-rule-micro-saas-startup-failure-analysis/); these percentages come from broader startup-failure literature (e.g., CB Insights post-mortem analyses of ~483+ startups), not indie/micro-SaaS specifically, so they characterize the startup failure-mode landscape generally rather than the indie segment precisely.

### Inferences
- The "heavy skew" claim is well supported directionally (multiple independent descriptions agree), but the *exact* shape (e.g., Pareto exponent, precise percentile cutoffs) is not something any source here measured rigorously — treat specific percentages (e.g., "top 5% capture 70%") as illustrative single-source estimates, not validated statistics.
- Success-story aggregators like Starter Story, by design, only sample the right tail of the distribution, which likely distorts public perception of what's "typical" (see survivorship-bias section below).

### Gaps
- No rigorous statistical distribution (e.g., a Gini coefficient, a fitted power-law exponent, a percentile table from a random or comprehensive sample) was found for indie/micro-SaaS revenue.
- No dataset was found that tracks the *same cohort* of makers over time to measure what fraction eventually succeed after multiple attempts (survivorship within an individual's own track record, as opposed to a single snapshot of products).

## Has the rise of AI coding tools measurably increased the number of people attempting this, and is there data on whether it increased success rates or just flooded the market?

### Takeaway
There is solid quantitative evidence that AI coding tools have sharply increased the *volume* of app/software submissions (a verifiable App Store statistic), but no credible quantitative evidence was found that this increased volume has improved success/revenue rates — if anything, commentary suggests the bottleneck (distribution, sales, product-market fit) is unchanged or worsened, and a widely-circulated growth figure turned out to be unsourced.

### Cited Findings
- Apple's App Store logged **557,000 new app submissions in 2025, a 24% increase from 2024** — described as the **first meaningful increase since 2016's all-time high of ~1 million apps** — [Appfigures Explorer data, via search summary](https://appfigures.com/resources/insights/20251205?f=2) (direct fetch of Appfigures was blocked by network egress; this is the search engine's paraphrase of Appfigures' published insight, so treat as a secondary citation of a primary industry-analytics source).
- Commentary attributes this surge to AI/"vibe coding" tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, Replit) lowering technical barriers, enabling non-technical people to ship functional apps "in days, not months" — [search summary of coverage, e.g. gadgethacks.com / techbuzz.ai](https://apple.gadgethacks.com/news/app-store-new-apps-surge-ai-coding-tools-behind-60-spike/).
- Apple's App Store review times reportedly ballooned from roughly 24 hours to as long as 30–45 days, attributed to the review-queue strain from the submission flood — [search summary, e.g. kkm-mako.com / gadgethacks.com](https://kkm-mako.com/en/blog/articles/app-store-review-delay-vibe-coding-crisis/).
- Important correction/fact-check found in the research: **a widely-circulating claim of an "84% annual surge" in App Store submissions attributed to vibe coding has no identifiable primary source** — no primary source reviewed supports the 84% figure, and the verified figures instead point to Appfigures' 24% year-over-year growth (with possibly higher isolated monthly spikes) — [thenextweb.com, via search summary](https://thenextweb.com/news/vibe-coding-apple-app-store-surge-crackdown) (direct fetch blocked; this fact-check itself is a secondary paraphrase, but it is methodologically important: it shows an unsourced statistic propagating through tech media, which is itself a data point about hype dynamics in this space).
- On the "does it help success rates" question: one case example cited a founder who built a micro-SaaS in **6 days** using AI tools and hit **$870 MRR**, but it took **three months to land the first 10 customers** — used by the source to argue the real bottleneck is sales/distribution, not development speed — [search summary citing buildmvpfast.com](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/ai-built-micro-saas-vs-traditional-saas-2026) (single anecdote, not a statistic).
- AI-agent/application-layer startup commentary (VC-focused, not micro-SaaS specific) notes "application-layer AI ... is seeing early signs of saturation," and that a "significant percentage of early-stage agent startups are projected to exhaust their capital reserves by late 2026" due to token costs and slow enterprise sales cycles — [search summary of VC-sector reporting](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/raising-seed-for-ai-agent-startup-2026) (this describes funded AI-agent startups, a different population from bootstrapped micro-SaaS, but is relevant context on "does more building activity translate into more success" — the answer trending negative even in the funded segment).

### Inferences
- The evidence supports "AI coding tools increased the number of attempts" (App Store submission volume is a fairly hard, verifiable number from Appfigures). 
- The evidence does **not** support "AI coding tools increased the success rate" — no source found measured post-AI-tools success/revenue rates against a pre-AI-tools baseline. The qualitative commentary (sales/distribution as the persistent bottleneck, product review backlogs, concerns about quality) leans toward the "flooded the market" interpretation rather than "raised the win rate," but this is an inference from directional commentary, not a measured comparison.
- The debunked "84% surge" figure is itself useful evidence for the report: it shows that alarmist/exciting statistics about the AI-driven indie-building boom can spread through tech media without a traceable primary source — a caution for readers relying on secondhand stats in this space.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares indie-hacker/micro-SaaS success rates (e.g., % reaching $1K MRR) before vs. after the mainstreaming of AI coding tools (roughly 2023 onward). This is the single biggest evidentiary gap for the "flooded market vs. genuinely more winners" question.
- No data found on abandonment/churn rates of AI-generated apps specifically (e.g., what fraction of AI-coded App Store submissions are deleted, unmaintained, or removed within a year) — this would be the most direct evidence for the "AI slop" critique but was not located.

## What do skeptics/critics say — the "anyone can build a SaaS now" narrative as overstated or a bubble?

### Takeaway
There is an active, vocal skeptical discourse (Hacker News threads, named engineers, dictionary-of-the-year selections) arguing that building has become trivially easy but distribution, trust, product-market fit, and code quality have not — and some explicitly warn of a coming reckoning ("vibe slop crisis," "SaaSpocalypse").

### Cited Findings
- Collins Dictionary named **"vibe coding"** its word of the year for 2025; Macquarie Dictionary, The Economist, and Merriam-Webster all separately selected **"slop"**/"AI slop" as a 2025 word of the year, defined by Macquarie as "low-quality content created by generative AI, often containing errors, and not requested by the user" — [phys.org / downtoearth.org.in, via search summary](https://phys.org/news/2025-12-slop-vibe-coding-glazing-ai.html). This is a strong, citable, quantifiable-in-kind signal (multiple independent dictionaries converging on the same critical vocabulary in the same year) that "AI slop" skepticism is mainstream, not fringe.
- Engineers **Mario Zechner and Armin Ronacher** are named as warning that "vibe slop" — low-quality AI-generated code — "poses rising risks for startups and investors," describing the rush to ship AI-written software as "creating a ticking time bomb of buggy, insecure code that could sink startups" — [cryptobriefing.com / medium summary, via search](https://medium.com/newsarticulated/the-ai-superstars-who-say-a-vibe-slop-crisis-is-coming-and-what-it-means-for-software-s-future-f276c4d875b2) (named, identifiable critics — a stronger form of evidence than an anonymous aggregator claim, though the original long-form essay itself was not directly fetched in this pass).
- On Hacker News, the thread **"Ask HN: If Everyone Can 'Build' a SaaS, What Becomes Valuable?"** (item 46791613) surfaces the core skeptical argument: as technical barriers collapse, commenters argue the scarce/valuable assets shift to **distribution, vertical-specific data/models, and integration & trust** — not the software itself. One highly-noted comment (user `spaceman_2020`) argues that unless the tech is genuinely revolutionary, "your biggest problem will be distribution and discovery, not tech." Another (user `codingdave`) argues that LLMs don't guarantee building the *correct* product or building it *sustainably*, so "this really doesn't change the overall market" — [news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46791613](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46791613).
- Other relevant Hacker News threads found (titles only, not deep-read in this pass, but indicative of an active critical discourse): **"Is it a bubble?"** (id=46220640), **"AI is killing B2B SaaS"** (id=46888441), **"AI agents are starting to eat SaaS"** (id=46268452), **"The AI bubble is popping; we just don't know it yet"** (id=49154601), and **"An AI agent coding skeptic tries AI agent coding, in excessive detail"** (id=47183527) — [news.ycombinator.com](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46220640) (titles/topics confirmed via search; full thread content not fetched, flagged as a gap for deeper synthesis).
- The term **"SaaSpocalypse"** has reportedly circulated across tech/VC/public-markets commentary to describe the narrative that AI agents and vibe coding are structurally changing (undermining) traditional per-seat SaaS economics — [thesaascfo.com, via search summary](https://www.thesaascfo.com/the-saaspocalypse-ai-agents-vibe-coding-and-the-changing-economics-of-saas/) (direct fetch blocked; only the search-summarized framing could be confirmed, not the full argument).
- A milder counter-framing also appears in commentary: "this confuses oversaturation in a few flashy categories with the death of the entire model, when the truth is messier than that" — i.e., some sources argue the "micro-SaaS is dead" narrative is itself overstated, suggesting the discourse contains both boom-skeptics and skeptics-of-the-skepticism — [search summary citing buildmvpfast.com](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/ai-built-micro-saas-vs-traditional-saas-2026).
- On the VC/funded side (adjacent but distinct population from bootstrapped micro-SaaS): commentary notes investor expectations tightened sharply — "In 2023, you could raise pre-product on an AI idea. In 2026, you need proof that someone will pay for it," with median AI-agent seed post-money cited at **$24M** — [search summary of VC-sector reporting](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/raising-seed-for-ai-agent-startup-2026) — relevant as evidence that even sophisticated capital allocators have grown more skeptical of the "easy AI startup" narrative over 2023–2026.

### Inferences
- The skepticism is not confined to obscure blog posts — it has reached the level of dictionary word-of-the-year selections across four independent dictionaries/publications in the same year, which is a meaningful, citable signal of how mainstream "AI slop" criticism has become by late 2025.
- The Hacker News discourse suggests a nuanced skeptical position rather than blanket dismissal: critics generally don't deny that *building* has gotten easier; they argue that *building* was never the bottleneck, and that AI tools have not solved (and may not be able to solve) distribution, trust, and product-market fit, which remain the actual constraints on success.

### Gaps
- Full text of the Zechner/Ronacher "vibe slop" essay was not directly retrieved (only secondary paraphrase) — a follow-up fetch of the original essay (likely on Zechner's or Ronacher's own blog) would strengthen this citation.
- Full text/vote-weighted top comments of the flagged HN threads ("Is it a bubble?", "AI is killing B2B SaaS", etc.) were not read in depth in this pass; only titles and one thread's summary were captured.
- No data found quantifying *how many* critics vs. enthusiasts exist (e.g., sentiment analysis of HN/Twitter discourse) — the skeptical viewpoint is evidenced qualitatively (named critics, prominent threads, dictionary selections) but not measured at scale.

## What survivorship-bias critique exists of the success-story genre?

### Takeaway
The survivorship-bias critique is explicitly and repeatedly named within the indie hacker community itself (including on Indie Hackers' own platform), with the core argument being that people who fail or quietly abandon projects don't publish retrospectives, while people who hit meaningful MRR do — systematically distorting the public's sense of typical outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- An Indie Hackers community post is explicitly titled **"Success stories suffer from survivorship bias"**, arguing that because "only the successful tell success stories," the visible narrative is a logical-fallacy-prone sample — [indiehackers.com](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/success-stories-suffer-from-survivorship-bias-e14d681b39) (direct fetch blocked by network egress in this pass; title and framing confirmed via search summary — the existence of this post, written by the community itself, is notable evidence that the survivorship-bias critique is self-aware within the indie hacking community, not just an outside critique).
- Related Indie Hackers community posts found with similar framing: **"I'm DONE with all these 'Success Stories' Online"** and **"Is your self-worth tied to MRR?"** — both suggest community-level fatigue with the curated-success narrative — [indiehackers.com](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/i-m-done-with-all-these-success-stories-online-46084c4296) (titles confirmed via search; full text not fetched).
- Direct quote captured via search summary: "People who fail, stall, or quietly abandon a product don't write a retrospective, while people who hit $50K MRR write a very detailed one. Failed ventures don't catch headlines, and stories about them will never see the light of day." — attributed to the same survivorship-bias discussion thread [as above] (paraphrase/summary-level citation, exact original wording not independently verified due to fetch restrictions).
- The individual four-year "26 projects, $115K, 18 of 26 made $0" ledger (cited above) is explicitly framed by its publisher as a **counter-example to the genre** — i.e., someone publishing their full failure-inclusive record specifically to push back against survivorship-biased narratives — [buildmvpfast.com summary](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/indie-hacking-real-numbers-4-years-26-projects-115k-2026).
- Starter Story's own platform statistics (4,483+ case studies, $10.8B+ aggregate revenue) function, by the research's assessment, as a real-world illustration of the critique in action: the platform's entire business model is built on showcasing revenue-generating founders, meaning its huge aggregate numbers cannot be read as representative of typical outcomes for people who "try" to build a business — [starterstory.com](https://www.starterstory.com/) (this is the research agent's inference/framing of Starter Story's own published numbers, not a claim made by Starter Story itself; flagged as inference, not a direct critique-source quote).

### Inferences
- The survivorship-bias critique is unusually well-evidenced for a "soft" methodological point: it is made by the community itself (Indie Hackers posts), illustrated by a named individual's full disclosure of failures (26-projects ledger), and indirectly demonstrated by the existence and business model of curated success-story platforms (Starter Story). Together these triangulate to a credible, multi-angle case that publicly visible success stories are a highly non-representative sample of outcomes.
- Combined with the "54% make $0" and "18 of 26 projects made $0" data points, the survivorship-bias critique is not just a rhetorical point — it is numerically consistent with the actual (if imperfectly measured) base rates found elsewhere in this research.

### Gaps
- No formal/academic treatment of survivorship bias specifically in the indie-hacker/micro-SaaS context was found (the concept is well established in finance/behavioral-economics literature generally, e.g., Nassim Taleb's writing, but this research did not locate an indie-SaaS-specific academic or rigorous journalistic long-form treatment — only community forum posts and blog summaries).
- Full original text of the "Success stories suffer from survivorship bias" Indie Hackers post could not be retrieved due to network egress restrictions on indiehackers.com during this research session; only its title, framing, and a paraphrased quote were obtainable via search-engine summarization.
